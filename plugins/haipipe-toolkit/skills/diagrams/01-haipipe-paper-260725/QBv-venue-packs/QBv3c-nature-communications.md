## Opening

This desk's test names a person who does not work in your field and asks whether they keep reading. It is the only bar in the tree that a paper cannot check against its own data. So how is it supposed to be scored?

**Where this page sits**: it is one outlet under `QBv3`, which owns what the Nature portfolio's five outlets share.
This page owns only what is true of `playbook-nature-portfolio/nature-communications/`.

**Why this is the portfolio's default rather than its specialist**: the four siblings each name a domain, and this one names an audience.
A paper is here when its finding travels and nowhere in the portfolio otherwise fits.

**What makes it hard to use**: breadth is a judgement about readers, and unlike a validation design or an identification strategy, nothing on disk settles it.

## Writing Style

How this page must be written. Read it before editing, and edit to it.

**Inherited from `QB4`**: the page grammar, the section order, and the sentence rules come from `QB4-overall.md` and are not restated here.

**Never transcribe the section norms**: word budgets live in `nature-communications/natcomm-<section>/style.md` and are cited, never copied.

**Keep the stranger in the sentence**: this desk's bar is about a specific imagined reader, and abstracting it into significance loses what makes it checkable at all.

✅ `would a researcher outside this subfield stop scrolling`  ❌ `is the finding broadly significant`

## Content

### 1 · An audience bar, not a domain bar

**The one outlet defined by who reads it**: which is why it is both the portfolio's default and its least decidable.

```text
  💡 what follows from naming an audience
     ── the abstract carries the whole test
     ── the first sentence is doing the work the four
        sibling desks spread across a Methods section
     ── a correct, well-validated, narrow paper FAILS here
        and passes elsewhere

  🚫 and the two technical rejections say the same thing twice
     "a technical method with no scientific finding"
     "scope too narrow for a broad readership"
     ── both are breadth, stated from different ends
```

🌍 Establishes the abstract as the artifact this desk actually scores, which is a section-level consequence of an outlet-level bar.

#### 1.1 · Definitive-scale replication is a fit signal, which is unusual
(most desks in this tree treat replication as a lesser contribution)
This desk names it as something an editor leans forward for, provided the scale is definitive.
That is a genuine route for a paper with large data and no novel mechanism, and no other outlet in the tree offers it.

### 2 · How to score a bar with no artifact behind it

**Nothing on disk settles breadth**: the closest available proxy is the exemplar set.

```text
  📊 15 exemplars ── the portfolio's smallest base
     ── and the only evidence available about what this
        desk considers broad

  🔍 what could make the bar checkable
     ── read the exemplars for what field each one crosses
     ── record the crossing, not the topic
     ── then a candidate is scored on whether it crosses
        anything at all

  💥 today: a human reads the sentence and decides
```

⚠️ Establishes the exemplar set as the only usable evidence for this bar, and reading it as the cheapest route to a check.

## Law

This is the one outlet in the tree whose bar names an audience rather than a domain, so the abstract carries the whole test and a correct, well-validated, narrow paper fails here while passing elsewhere.
A bar with no artifact behind it is scored against exemplars or it is not scored at all.

## Glossary

- **Audience bar**: a desk requirement stated in terms of who reads the paper rather than what field it is in, unique to this outlet in the venue tree.
- **Definitive-scale replication**: a replication whose scale settles the question, named here as a fit signal and nowhere else in the tree.
